# One observation * A dot is a measurement: an input and an output, each with its unit. * Same dot, different domains: age/height, time/power, altitude/temperature. * A second measurement, later in the input. * Drop the units: the whole task is a set of such pairs.
# Several outputs per task * A new dataset: same tasks, two quantities measured on each. * The second output is costly, so it is observed sparsely -- and stops two thirds of the way through the domain. * Two wishes: interpolate the expensive output, and use its relation to the cheap one to forecast where it was never measured.
# Recovering a terrain from passes * Each pass is one task: a line of noisy, biased observations across the map. * No pass sees the whole terrain, yet together they recover the average topography. * The posterior variance says where to send the next pass.
# Who am I? * Me, then the people this work is shared with, left to right. * Namur, BIRA-IASB, Paris-Saclay, Toulouse, INRAe. * MagmaClustR came first; MIMOSA is where it went. * What MIMOSA buys you, in five lines.
# Machine learning as a whole * Everything here rests on inductive reasoning. * From particular observations to general rules, keeping the most probable ones.
# Hypothesis space, learning and uncertainty * F is every function from inputs to outputs; f* is the one that generated the data. * A model assumption carves out H, the predictors we can actually reach. * Learning walks h_0 to the fitted h-hat; h* is the best H has to offer. * Each hop is a source of error. Optimisation aside, they add up to what we observe. * Iris petals: a spherical mixture stalls in a local optimum, two components on setosa. * A luckier run reaches h*; only a richer covariance moves h* closer to f*.
# The Bayes rule * Parameters theta, dataset D. * Posterior, likelihood, prior, evidence. * The evidence is a constant in theta: drop it, keep a proportionality. * Left: no prior, parameters are fixed values pinned down by enough data. * Right: a prior over the parameters, updated point by point into a posterior.
# The five levels * Two floors: parameters theta, hyperparameters Phi above them. * On each floor, either pick one best value or keep them all and integrate. * MAP is MLE plus a prior; MAP-II is type-II MLE plus a prior, one floor up. * Type-II MLE is plain maximum likelihood run on Phi, once theta is gone. * The integral is what makes a level bayesian, not the maximisation. * Full Bayes stops optimising altogether: no closed form, MCMC or variational.
# Characteristics of functional data * The object of interest is a function: defined everywhere, infinite-dimensional. * What we get is a handful of points, at inputs we did not choose, with noisy outputs. * Two sampling grids never match, and no sample ever pins the function down.
# GP from first principles * Two measurements of one swimmer: what happens in between? * A mean that goes from one to the other, so the prediction is correlated to both. * Uncertainty grows with the distance to the points. * More points to the left: the trend now depends on many of them, not just the closest. * Far from any data there is nothing left to correlate to -- only prior knowledge. * The forecast falls back on the mean of every swimmer, with the prior's uncertainty.
# Kernel design * The kernel is where the shape of the prior lives. * Squared exponential: infinitely smooth, everything within a length-scale looks alike. * Matérn 5/2, 3/2, 1/2: the same idea, rougher and rougher. * Linear: covariance grows with the inputs themselves, samples are straight lines. * Periodic: correlation comes back at every period. * Kernels add and multiply, and the sum or product is a kernel again.
# Model selection * The three things we needed become two objects: a mean function and a kernel. * Read the prior at two inputs only: the pair of values is a bivariate normal. * The tilt of the cloud is the kernel value between the two inputs. * Move the second input away and the correlation, hence the tilt, goes to zero. * Any finite set of inputs gives a normal, so the data has a density. * Three terms: how well it fits, how complex the covariance is, and a constant. * That density scores a length-scale: too wiggly is penalised, so is too smooth. * The optimum is the length-scale used on the previous slides.
# When the likelihood lies * Same career, same constant mean, now with all three hyperparameters free. * Start from a poor guess on the left: the climb leads away from the good solution. * Left end: the signal variance has collapsed, every point is noise around the mean. * Right end: the length-scale has fallen below the spacing, the noise has collapsed instead, and the fit spikes to each point and forgets it immediately. * Both are real optima: the gradient vanishes there, they are not failures to converge. * The valley between them runs far below the panel, so no optimiser crosses it. * Start anywhere reasonable and you land on the middle solution instead.
# Where are the parameters? * Ask the room first: on which line of the table are we? * The prediction is a linear model: the weights are the vectors and matrices. * The mean and kernel functions only say how to fill those weights in. * The constant, the length-scale, the variance and the noise are one floor up. * So: type-II MLE, aka empirical Bayes. * The weights are never optimised, and there are infinitely many of them: GPs are infinite-dimensional, non-parametric models. * If a Gaussian process is a car, the MVN is its engine.
# What can it do? * Monthly mean CO2 at Mauna Loa, the longest direct record we have. * Everything before 2010 is given to the model, the rest is kept back. * The kernel is written down first: trend, seasonal cycle, irregularities, noise. * Nothing else is told to the model -- no trend removal, no seasonal adjustment. * The fit goes through the data and the forecast keeps the seasonal cycle. * Sixteen years later the truth sits at the edge of the band: the trend accelerated.
# Why did I call it useless? * The good first: a GP hands back a distribution, not a point. * Noise and lack of data are two different things, and it tells them apart. * That is why it turns up as a component inside bigger methods. * Now the price: superb between the points, much weaker past them. * Cholesky is cubic -- the model dies on large datasets. * And nothing in it says what to do with many series at once.
# Throwback to the hypothesis space * Same picture as in chapter 2: F is every function, H is what the model can reach. * For Gaussian processes H is tiny -- most tasks sit far outside it. * It is not that we cannot know; it is that the model cannot approach f*. * But inside that small region, a GP is exactly the right tool.
# The generative model * One observation is a global trend, a task-specific deviation and noise. * Each of the three terms gets its own prior: two GPs and a Gaussian. * The mean process varies more than the tasks that ride on it. * One draw of the mean process, one task sampled at 25 inputs, then noise. * The observation is what you get by stacking the three bands on top of each other. * Every other task rides on the same mean process, on its own inputs. * The prior on the mean process is not tied to one cluster: draw it again and the same machinery gives a second population of observations.
# The parameters to define * The generative model in full: a latent assignment, a cluster mean process, a task deviation and noise. * Six quantities to define or optimise, one line each. * Two clusters, their mean processes dashed, the tasks riding on them. * The hyper-prior mean starts at zero, then constant, then affine -- shared by both clusters. * Cluster kernel: variance sets how far the two mean processes wander apart, length-scale how smoothly. * Task kernel: same two knobs, applied to the deviation of each task. * Mixture proportions decide how many tasks land in each cluster. * Noise is the last term, and the only one that is not a process.
# The configurations * Four flags, each one a constraint the framework can drop. * Isotopic tasks: every task observed at the same inputs. * Shared cluster hyper-parameters off: each cluster gets its own prior mean, then its own variance and length-scale. * Cluster hyper-parameters in tasks: the task kernel differs from one cluster to the other. * Shared task hyper-parameters off: every task gets its own kernel.
# Several channels per task * Every task is measured on two channels at the same inputs. * The top channel separates the two populations; the bottom one does not, its tasks wandering further than its mean-processes are apart. * Fitted together, the two channels share one set of cluster assignments: the top channel is what makes the clustering sharp. * A new task arrives with the bottom channel only -- the ambiguous one. * It still gets cluster coefficients, because the clusters were learnt from both channels at once. * And with a cluster comes a prediction for the channel it never measured: the mean-process of its cluster, widened by the task variance.
# Multi-output kernels * Three families, one prior draw of two outputs each, same random draw throughout: what changes between the columns is only how the family ties the outputs together. * ICM: one shared kernel, one coregionalisation matrix. * LCM: a sum of such terms, each with its own kernel. * Process convolution: one latent field, one smoothing window per output. * Adding a latent process frees the second output from the first; the convolution family has no such knob. * Both the mean-process and the task deviation can take any of the four, and the pairing is a hypothesis about the data, not a default.
# Early prediction of child obesity * 1177 children of the GUSTO cohort, birth to 10 years, one task each, measured at their own visit dates -- a fully heterotopic grid. * The clinical question is the line: overweight at 10 (22.4 BMI, sex-specific in the fit). * Five mean-processes. They agree on infancy and on the rebound, and separate after it. * One girl, three years of measurements: the draws already put some mass above the line. * Right: the whole test set. Every child's risk (blue = fine at 10, red = overweight), and the five scores at the study's 5% flag. * Stepping the context one year at a time: her fan rises, her dot rises with it, and the specificity is what improves -- the model rules overweight *out* long before it rules it in.
# Trajectory clustering around a roundabout * Every car crossing the roundabout is one task: its x and its y are two *channels*, sampled at the same normalised times. * As trajectories, the data is a picture of the roundabout. Split into components, it is two ordinary functional datasets -- and the clusters are plain to see. * Six mean-processes, one per route through the roundabout, fitted on both channels at once. * A car seen only up to the middle of its pass: the mixture splits evenly over two routes, and the samples show what that means -- we do not know which exit it takes. * Another car, whose y sensor failed: x alone pins the mixture on one route. * With the route known, y follows -- the mean-process of that cluster, widened by the task variance -- and pairing samples of it with the measured x rebuilds a trajectory from a half-dead sensor.
# One swimmer, two pool sizes * 128 French swimmers, 100 m freestyle, one task per swimmer, one colour each. * Two clusters, and they are the two genders -- the model is told which. * The same swimmers race in 50 m pools too: a second output, observed on other days. * One of them: a long, fast career in the 25 m pool, and no Olympic-size race at all. How fast would she be over 50 m? * The draws are not centred on her cluster's mean-process: her own 25 m deviation carries over to the other pool through the task kernel. * Add a few of her actual 50 m races and the draws collapse onto them.
